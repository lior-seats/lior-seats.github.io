Why cron expressions are hard to read

A cron expression looks deceptively simple. Five space-separated fields, each controlling a unit of time. But the combination of special characters — asterisks, slashes, commas, hyphens, and sometimes question marks — makes even experienced developers pause when reading an expression they didn't write themselves.

0 3 * * 1

Is that "every Monday at 3:00 AM" or "the 1st of every month at 3:00 AM"? (It's Monday — field 5 is day-of-week, not day-of-month.) Now try this one:

*/15 9-17 * * 1-5

Every 15 minutes, between 9am and 5pm, Monday through Friday. Reasonable to figure out — but not instantly obvious. The moment you add ranges, step values, and multiple comma-separated values, the cognitive overhead compounds fast.

The five cron fields explained

A standard cron expression has exactly five fields, separated by spaces:

Field Position Range Example
Minute 1st 0–59 30 = at minute 30
Hour 2nd 0–23 3 = 3 AM
Day of month 3rd 1–31 1 = 1st of month
Month 4th 1–12 */3 = every 3 months
Day of week 5th 0–7 (0 and 7 = Sunday) 1-5 = Mon–Fri

The field order is always the same: minute, hour, day-of-month, month, day-of-week. A useful mnemonic: M H D M D — minute, hour, day, month, day-of-week.

Special characters

  • * — every value in this field ("every minute", "every hour", etc.)
  • */n — every nth value (e.g. */5 in the minute field = every 5 minutes)
  • a-b — a range from a to b inclusive (e.g. 9-17 in the hour field = 9am through 5pm)
  • a,b,c — a list of specific values (e.g. 1,15 in day-of-month = 1st and 15th)
The day-of-week confusion On most systems, both 0 and 7 mean Sunday. So 0-5 means Sunday through Friday, not Monday through Friday. To get Monday–Friday, use 1-5. This trips up many developers who expect 0-indexed Monday.

Common cron expression patterns

Here are the most frequently used patterns and what they mean:

Expression Meaning
* * * * * Every minute
0 * * * * Every hour (at :00)
0 0 * * * Every day at midnight
0 3 * * * Every day at 3:00 AM
0 0 * * 0 Every Sunday at midnight
0 9 * * 1-5 Weekdays at 9:00 AM
0 0 1 * * First of every month at midnight
*/15 * * * * Every 15 minutes
0 */6 * * * Every 6 hours (at :00)
30 9 1,15 * * 9:30 AM on the 1st and 15th of each month

Cron syntax varies by platform

Standard 5-field cron expressions work in Linux crontab, most Unix systems, and most job schedulers. But some platforms have their own variations — and knowing the difference can save you a frustrating debugging session.

GitHub Actions

GitHub Actions uses the same 5-field format with a UTC timezone. Note: GitHub documentation warns that scheduled workflows may run up to 15 minutes late during periods of high load. */5 in the minute field is the minimum interval GitHub allows.

on:
  schedule:
    - cron: '0 3 * * 1'  # Monday at 3:00 AM UTC

AWS EventBridge (CloudWatch Events)

AWS uses a 6-field cron format with an additional year field at the end, and uses ? as a wildcard for either day-of-month or day-of-week (not both can be specified). Their expressions look like:

cron(0 3 ? * MON *)   # Monday at 3:00 AM UTC, every year

AWS also supports named days (MON, TUE, etc.) and months (JAN, FEB, etc.) in addition to numeric values.

Kubernetes CronJob

Kubernetes uses standard 5-field cron syntax. The schedule field follows Unix cron conventions exactly. Timezones can be specified with a timeZone field in the spec (since Kubernetes 1.25).

Heroku Scheduler

Heroku Scheduler uses a simplified interface — every 10 minutes, every hour, or daily at a specific UTC time. It does not support arbitrary cron expressions.

How to validate a cron expression before deploying

The best practice before deploying any cron job is to validate the expression in three ways:

  1. Parse the expression to plain English — confirm it means what you think it means
  2. Check the next N run times — look at 5–10 upcoming runs to verify the pattern matches your intent, especially for complex expressions
  3. Consider timezone — cron runs in the server's system timezone unless specified otherwise. A "3:00 AM" job on a server in UTC fires at 8:00 PM Pacific or 11:00 PM Eastern. Always confirm what timezone your scheduler uses.

The midnight trap 0 0 * * * runs at midnight server time — which may not be midnight in your timezone or your users' timezone. If you're running a report "for yesterday's data," running at midnight UTC means it runs mid-afternoon Pacific — potentially before the business day ends. Always be explicit about which midnight you mean.

After validation: monitoring your cron jobs

Validating the expression before deploy is step one. But once a cron job is running, a whole different class of problems can emerge: the job starts but fails partway through, auto-renewal processes stop running, or a scheduler itself goes down. Syntax validation doesn't catch runtime failures.

The standard pattern for detecting missed cron runs is a dead man's switch: your job pings a monitoring service on successful completion, and the service alerts you if the ping doesn't arrive within the expected window. One curl command at the end of your job is all it takes.
