Free SSL Certificate Monitoring: 5 Ways to Know Before Your Cert Expires

Your SSL certificate expired at 2am on a Tuesday. Users saw "Your connection is not private." Support queues filled before anyone noticed.

SSL certificate expiration is one of the most preventable outages in software. The expiry date is stamped in the certificate itself — you can read it at any time. And yet teams get caught by it constantly, because there's no built-in alarm. The cert silently ticks toward expiration, and the first signal most teams get is a browser error page.

This article covers five free approaches to SSL certificate monitoring, from quick manual checks to automated alerting, including our free SSLWatch tool that requires no signup to use.

Why SSL expiry keeps catching teams off guard

Let's Encrypt certificates expire every 90 days. Traditional CA certificates typically expire every 1–2 years. In either case, the failure mode is the same: the renewal process breaks at some point — a cron job that stopped running, an ACME challenge that started failing, a DNS change that broke auto-renewal — and nobody notices until the cert is already expired.

The problem is compounded by two factors:

  • Auto-renewal breeds complacency. Teams set up Certbot, confirm auto-renewal works, and never check again. When auto-renewal silently breaks six months later, there's no alert.
  • Wildcard certs mask subdomain risk. A team monitoring example.com may forget that api.example.com has its own cert on a different renewal schedule.

Here are five free methods to close the gap.

Method 1: Browser certificate inspector

Every browser shows certificate details including the expiry date. In Chrome: click the padlock icon → Connection is secure → Certificate is valid. In Firefox: padlock → Connection secure → More information → View Certificate.

This works for one-off checks, but it requires someone to remember to check, which is why it fails in practice. Use it as a quick diagnostic when you suspect an issue — not as ongoing monitoring.

# Quick check from the terminal — shows notAfter date
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -dates

This prints the notBefore and notAfter dates for the certificate currently served. Run it on any domain to see exactly when it expires.

Method 2: Google Search Console alerts

Google Search Console sends alerts when it encounters a security issue on your property — including certificate errors. If your cert expires and Googlebot hits the site, you'll receive an email under Security Issues in Search Console.

The limitation: the alert arrives when Googlebot crawls the page after the expiry, not before. This is a reactive notification, not a proactive one. For high-traffic sites that Googlebot crawls frequently, you might hear about it within hours of expiry. For low-traffic sites, it may take days.

Google Search Console is worth having regardless — it covers many security issue types beyond SSL — but it should not be your primary SSL expiry monitor.

Method 3: Let's Encrypt expiry emails

If you issued your certificate through Let's Encrypt, they send expiry reminder emails at 20 days and 7 days before expiration — but only if auto-renewal hasn't already renewed the cert. The email goes to the address you provided during the initial ACME registration.

Check which email your certs are registered under:

# Find the registered email for a Certbot-managed cert
sudo certbot certificates

This works reliably for domains managed by Certbot, but has two gaps:

  • If auto-renewal partially works (cert renewed but not deployed), you won't get an alert and the expired cert stays in production
  • It only covers Let's Encrypt certs — any cert from a traditional CA, AWS ACM, or other issuer is not covered

What to do right now

If you're not currently monitoring SSL expiry, here's a quick triage:

  1. Run the openssl command from Method 1 against your top 5 domains and subdomains. Note which expire soonest.
  2. If any cert expires within 30 days: renew it now and verify the renewal is actually being served.
  3. Set up at least one automated monitor (Method 4 or 5) so you're not relying on memory.
  4. Verify your Let's Encrypt auto-renewal is working: sudo certbot renew --dry-run. If it fails, fix it before the cert expires.
  5. Add all production subdomains to your monitoring list, not just the apex domain.

The entire triage takes under 20 minutes and eliminates a class of outage that's entirely preventable.
