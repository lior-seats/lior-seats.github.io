Heroku Scheduler Alternatives in 2026: Migrate Your Cron Jobs

cron monitoring heroku October 2026

Heroku Scheduler was simple: add a one-liner, pick a frequency (every 10 minutes, hourly, or daily), and forget about it. Until you couldn't, because Heroku's free tier disappeared in November 2022—and with it, the zero-cost scheduler that hundreds of thousands of small projects relied on.

If you're still running scheduled tasks on Heroku or just migrated, you need a replacement. But there's a second problem most migration guides miss: your new scheduler also needs monitoring. Heroku's dashboard at least showed you recent runs. Most replacements give you nothing.

This guide covers both: the best alternatives for running cron jobs in 2026, and how to make sure you know when they stop.

Why Developers Miss Heroku Scheduler

Heroku Scheduler had three things going for it:

  • Zero configuration — no YAML, no workflows, no separate infrastructure
  • Attached to your dyno — ran in the same environment as your app, with all your env vars automatically
  • Free — it never cost anything on the free tier

The weaknesses: it only supported 10-minute, hourly, and daily frequencies (no arbitrary cron expressions), it had no retry logic, and failures were largely invisible unless you were watching logs.

Most alternatives fix the frequency problem. Few fix the visibility problem.

The Best Heroku Scheduler Alternatives

1. Render Cron Jobs

If you migrated from Heroku to Render (a common path), Render has a first-class Cron Jobs feature. You define a service, pick a cron schedule (full 5-field expressions supported), and Render runs it on their infrastructure.

  • Pricing: Free tier available; paid from $7/month for reliable execution
  • Best for: Teams already on Render
  • Limitation: No built-in failure alerting — you get logs, not alerts

2. Railway Cron

Railway has a cron job primitive that runs a command on a schedule. Configuration is simple and it supports full cron expressions. Like Render, it's tightly integrated with your deployed services.

  • Pricing: Pay-as-you-go; very cheap for infrequent jobs
  • Best for: Projects already on Railway
  • Limitation: No failure alerting out of the box

3. GitHub Actions Scheduled Workflows

GitHub Actions supports cron schedules via the schedule trigger. It's free for public repos and included in most GitHub plans. For jobs that are loosely coupled from your app (data exports, cleanup scripts, reports), this is a solid zero-dependency option.

on:
  schedule:
    - cron: '0 8 * * 1-5'  # 8am weekdays UTC
  • Pricing: Free on public repos; included minutes on paid plans
  • Best for: Scripts that can live in a repo
  • Gotcha: GitHub Actions scheduled workflows can stop silently if the repo is inactive — a known limitation

4. AWS EventBridge Scheduler

AWS EventBridge Scheduler is the enterprise-grade option. It supports one-time and recurring schedules, scales to millions of invocations, and integrates natively with Lambda, ECS, SQS, and hundreds of other AWS services.

  • Pricing: ~$1 per million invocations (essentially free at small scale)
  • Best for: Lambda functions, ECS tasks, AWS-native workloads
  • Limitation: More setup; requires AWS account

5. Fly.io Machines (with cron)

Fly.io doesn't have a first-class cron feature, but you can run a lightweight cron container using a Docker image with crond or a similar scheduler baked in. It's more DIY but gives you full control.

  • Best for: Teams on Fly.io who want to keep everything in one place
  • Limitation: Requires building and maintaining a cron container

6. EasyCron

EasyCron is a pure-play hosted cron service — you give it a URL to hit, it hits it on a schedule. If your cron jobs are HTTP-triggered (common in Rails, Django, and Laravel apps), this is dead simple.

  • Pricing: Free tier (limited); $12/year for basic paid plan
  • Best for: HTTP-triggered jobs that were triggered by Heroku Scheduler hitting an endpoint
  • Limitation: Only works for HTTP-based jobs

7. Temporal / Inngest (for complex workflows)

If your cron jobs do more than simple scripted tasks — if they orchestrate steps, retry on failure, or fan out to parallel work — Temporal and Inngest are worth a look. They're overkill for a "clean up old rows" job but powerful for anything with real complexity.

The Hidden Problem: Your New Scheduler Has No Monitor

Here's what all the options above have in common: when your job stops running, nothing tells you.

With Heroku, you at least had the dashboard. With your new scheduler, you have logs — which you'll check when something is visibly broken, not when a silent job quietly stopped firing three weeks ago.

The pattern that fixes this is a heartbeat monitor (also called a dead man's switch). Instead of the monitor checking whether your job ran, your job checks in with the monitor. If a check-in doesn't arrive within the expected window, you get an alert.

The integration is a single curl call at the end of your job:

# Add to the end of any cron job
curl -s https://cronwatch.io/api/heartbeat/your-token

If that line doesn't execute — because the job failed, errored, or never started — the monitor alerts you.

This pattern works with every scheduler above: Render, Railway, GitHub Actions, AWS EventBridge, Fly.io, EasyCron, whatever you end up using.

Summary

If you were on Heroku Scheduler and need a drop-in replacement:

  • Already on Render/Railway? Use their native cron feature. It's the closest to what Heroku gave you.
  • Running AWS Lambda? EventBridge Scheduler is the right tool.
  • Script lives in a GitHub repo? GitHub Actions scheduled workflows are free and easy.
  • HTTP-triggered job? EasyCron or a cron-friendly PaaS works well.

Whichever option you pick, add a heartbeat monitor so you find out when it stops — before your users do.
