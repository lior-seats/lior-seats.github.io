DNS Monitoring Service: How to Monitor Your DNS Records 24/7

What to look for, what options exist, and why most teams don't have this set up yet.

DNS is infrastructure that mostly works until it doesn't — and when it doesn't, the consequences are immediate and broad. A changed A record pulls production traffic to the wrong server. A dropped MX record bounces all inbound email. A modified TXT record breaks DMARC enforcement and gets your outbound email flagged as spam.

A DNS monitoring service checks your records on a continuous schedule and alerts you the moment anything changes. This post covers what to look for in a monitoring service, what the real-world options are, and how to start monitoring your critical domains today.

What a DNS monitoring service actually does

At its core, a DNS monitoring service does three things:

  1. Takes a baseline snapshot of your DNS records when you first add a domain
  2. Polls your records repeatedly — typically every 5–60 minutes — querying from external resolvers so it sees what the real world sees, not what your internal network sees
  3. Alerts you with a diff when any record deviates from the baseline, showing old value → new value

The critical word is "diff." A service that only tells you "something changed" without showing you what changed is not useful — you still have to manually check every record to figure out what's different. The before/after comparison is what turns a vague alert into an actionable one.

What to monitor and why

What separates a good DNS monitoring service from a bad one

Query from multiple resolvers

If the monitoring service queries only from one location or uses your own DNS resolver, it can miss propagation issues. A record might appear correct on Cloudflare's resolver but still serve the old value to 30% of users via their ISP's cached resolver. Good monitoring services query from geographically distributed resolvers — at minimum, from multiple continents.

Alert latency

A service that checks every 60 minutes can leave you with 59 minutes of undetected downtime. For production domains, a check interval of 5–15 minutes is reasonable. Faster is better for high-traffic domains; slower is acceptable for low-traffic or non-critical subdomains.

False positive rate

TTL-based changes are normal. If your CDN dynamically updates A records with short TTLs as part of load balancing, a naive monitoring service will fire alerts constantly. A good service can be configured with a baseline tolerance — don't alert on changes to records you've explicitly flagged as dynamic.

Before/after diff in the alert

Worth repeating: the alert should show you the old value and the new value side by side. Without this, every alert requires a manual investigation before you can even decide whether it's an incident. With it, you can decide in 10 seconds: "That's the CNAME I updated yesterday — expected" versus "That A record changed and I didn't touch it — incident."

Options worth knowing about

Purpose-built DNS change monitors

Several indie tools focus specifically on DNS change detection. They're typically low-cost ($5–15/month) and cover the basics: multiple domains, email alerts with diffs, configurable record types. The tradeoff is usually limited integrations — email-only, no Slack or PagerDuty without custom webhooks.

Uptime monitoring services with DNS features

Uptime Robot, Better Uptime, and similar services have added DNS monitoring as a feature alongside HTTP uptime checks. If you're already paying for uptime monitoring, check whether your plan includes DNS monitoring — you may already have access. The DNS monitoring in these tools tends to be basic (often just A record checking) but is better than nothing.

Your DNS provider's native audit log

Cloudflare, AWS Route53, and Google Cloud DNS all maintain audit logs of DNS changes. These are excellent for forensics — you can see who changed what and when — but they don't alert you in real time. They also only cover changes made within that provider's system; a change at the registrar level won't appear in your CDN's audit log.

Rolling your own

A bash script running on a cron, comparing dig output to a stored snapshot, is a fully functional DNS monitor for 1–2 critical records. It costs nothing beyond server time. It scales poorly — maintaining a custom monitor for 10 domains across 6 record types each is a maintenance task that eventually gets neglected. Use this approach for a handful of the most critical records while a proper service covers the rest.

Common failure mode The team sets up DNS monitoring for the main domain but not for subdomains. The incident comes from a staging subdomain that was pointed at a deprecated server and nobody noticed because nobody was watching it.

How to set up baseline monitoring in 10 minutes

  1. Check your current records. Use the free DNS checker to pull all A, MX, TXT, CNAME, NS, and SOA records for your primary domain. This is your baseline snapshot.
  2. Identify the critical records. For most production domains: the A record, MX records, and any TXT records containing SPF or DMARC data.
  3. Pick a monitoring interval. 15 minutes for production domains; 60 minutes for staging.
  4. Set up an alert destination. Email is the minimum. If you have an on-call rotation, wire it into your alerting system.
  5. Test the alert. Actually verify the alert fires by temporarily changing a test record in a staging environment. If the monitor doesn't catch it, your configuration is wrong.
