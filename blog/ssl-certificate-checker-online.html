What is an SSL certificate — and why does it expire?

An SSL/TLS certificate is a cryptographic credential that does two things: it proves your server is who it claims to be, and it enables HTTPS encryption between the browser and your server. Without a valid certificate, modern browsers show a hard warning that blocks most visitors from reaching your site.

Certificates expire by design. The maximum validity period has been progressively shortened by browsers and certificate authorities over the years — it was 5 years, then 2 years, then 1 year, and Apple has proposed a 47-day maximum. Short lifetimes reduce the window of exposure if a private key is compromised.

The practical consequence: every certificate you issue will eventually expire, and you must renew it before that happens.

What happens when an SSL certificate expires?

The moment an SSL certificate passes its expiry date, every major browser — Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge — immediately shows a full-page certificate error. Visitors see "Your connection is not private" or "This site can't provide a secure connection." Most users do not click through. Your site effectively goes down for new visitors.

Search engines also detect expired certificates. A site that stays expired for more than a few hours can see crawling halt and rankings drop as Google's crawler stops indexing new content.

Three common failure scenarios:

Auto-renewal silently fails

Let's Encrypt certificates are typically renewed by a certbot cron job or an ACME client running on your server. If that job fails — the server is rebooted, a permission changes, a port 80 challenge can't complete — the renewal doesn't happen. The certificate expires. You find out when a user reports the error, usually at 2am.

A certificate is on an overlooked subdomain

Large organizations manage dozens or hundreds of subdomains. Staging, internal tools, partner portals, legacy marketing pages — each one has its own certificate. It's easy for one to slip through when the team that provisioned it moves on or the renewal process isn't centralized.

CDN and load balancer certs are invisible

If you use a CDN (Cloudflare, Fastly, AWS CloudFront) or a load balancer (AWS ALB, GCP HTTPS LB), the certificate may be managed by the CDN rather than your server. These can and do expire if the auto-renewal fails, and the failure often isn't surfaced in your normal deployment tooling.

The 30-day rule If your certificate has fewer than 30 days remaining, you should renew it now — not when it expires. Renewal processes can fail, and you need time to recover. Most monitoring tools alert at 30 days and again at 7 days.

Check via command line (for reference)

If you prefer the terminal, here's the one-liner to check SSL expiry:

# Check expiry date for any domain
echo | openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -dates

# Output:
# notBefore=Jan  1 00:00:00 2026 GMT
# notAfter=Mar 31 23:59:59 2026 GMT

The notAfter field is your expiry date. This works from any Unix-like system with OpenSSL installed — which is most Linux servers and macOS.

What details does an SSL certificate checker show?

A complete SSL checker should show more than just the expiry date. Here's what matters:

  • Days remaining — the clearest signal. Green above 30, yellow below 30, red below 7.
  • Certificate issuer — which CA signed it (Let's Encrypt, DigiCert, Sectigo, ZeroSSL, etc.)
  • Subject and SAN — which domains and wildcards the cert is valid for. Misconfigured SANs cause browser errors even on a valid cert.
  • Chain validity — whether the full certificate chain (leaf → intermediate → root) is correctly presented. A broken chain causes trust errors in some browsers and clients.
  • Protocol versions — whether the server supports TLS 1.2 / 1.3 and has deprecated TLS 1.0/1.1.

How often should you check SSL certificates?

A one-time manual check is useful for diagnosing an issue, but it doesn't protect you going forward. Certificates expire on a fixed date regardless of whether you remember to check. The right approach is automated monitoring that alerts you before expiry.

For most teams, a useful monitoring cadence is:

  • Alert at 30 days remaining — gives you time to investigate and retry if renewal fails
  • Alert at 14 days remaining — second warning, renewal should be happening soon
  • Alert at 7 days remaining — urgent; act immediately
  • Alert at expiry — incident: site is down for HTTPS users

If you're using Let's Encrypt with certbot, the default auto-renews at 30 days but you still need to know if the renewal process fails. The only way to know is monitoring.
