Why developers are blocking AI crawlers

Three reasons come up repeatedly:

Bandwidth costs

AI crawlers — especially meta-externalagent and Bytespider — have been the dominant driver of unexpected hosting bill spikes for independent developers. They crawl aggressively, often hitting pages at rates that resemble a small DDoS. On a pay-per-GB hosting plan, the cost is real and immediate.

Training data control

Your content feeds LLM training datasets whether you want it to or not, unless you explicitly opt out. robots.txt is the standard, documented mechanism for doing so. Major AI labs have publicly committed to honoring it — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Apple all have. For well-behaved bots, it works.

No reciprocal value

Search crawlers index your content and drive traffic back to you. AI crawlers take content and surface it in chat interfaces that remove the user's need to ever visit your site. The value exchange is asymmetric. Many developers have concluded there's simply no reason to opt in.

What robots.txt can and cannot do for AI bots

robots.txt is a declaration of intent, not a technical barrier. The protocol works by convention: bots fetch your /robots.txt file before crawling and respect the rules it contains — if they choose to.

What it reliably does:

  • Blocks well-behaved bots that honor the Robots Exclusion Protocol (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Apple, Cohere, Amazon)
  • Provides a documented, standardized signal of your preferences
  • Is sufficient for the majority of AI crawler traffic in practice

What it cannot do:

  • Block crawlers that ignore it — notably Bytespider (ByteDance), which has documented history of crawling disallowed paths
  • Stop bots that mask their User-Agent or route through residential proxies
  • Retroactively remove content already scraped before you added rules
Bytespider requires server-level blocking Multiple independent researchers have confirmed Bytespider crawls pages explicitly disallowed in robots.txt. For reliable protection against it, add a firewall or CDN rule in addition to your robots.txt directive. Your CDN's bot management feature or a simple User-Agent block in Nginx/Apache is the practical solution.

For the vast majority of AI crawler traffic, robots.txt is the right first step and handles the bots most likely to be causing problems.

Manual directives for key bots

If you prefer to write or edit your robots.txt by hand, here are the exact directives for the bots most commonly cited in bandwidth spike reports:

Block GPTBot (OpenAI training crawler)

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

Block ClaudeBot (Anthropic)

Block both User-Agents — Anthropic uses both, and the legacy one still shows up in logs:

User-agent: ClaudeBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: anthropic-ai
Disallow: /

Block meta-externalagent (Meta)

This one is most frequently associated with hosting bill spikes. Block it explicitly — don't rely on a wildcard User-agent: * rule to catch it:

User-agent: meta-externalagent

Verify your rules are working

After deploying your robots.txt, verify the live file is what you intended. Three things silently break robots.txt updates:

  • CDN caching serving the old version
  • Deploying to the wrong path (file needs to be at /robots.txt, not /static/robots.txt)
  • A subtle syntax error causing parsers to skip your new rules
