The DNS record types you actually need to know

A record — maps domain to IPv4 address

The A record is the most fundamental DNS record. It answers the question: "What IP address does this domain point to?" When a browser navigates to example.com, it resolves the A record to get an IP, then connects to that IP over port 80 or 443.

When to look it up:

  • Domain isn't resolving or is resolving to the wrong server
  • You're migrating to a new server and want to verify the change propagated
  • You suspect a DNS hijack (A record pointing to an unexpected IP)
  • You need the raw IP for a firewall rule or monitoring probe

AAAA record — maps domain to IPv6 address

Same as the A record but for IPv6. Most modern stacks configure both A and AAAA. If you're seeing connectivity issues from IPv6-native networks, check whether your AAAA record points to a reachable server. A common misconfiguration is having an AAAA record pointing to an address that no longer exists after a server migration — IPv6 users get a connection refused while IPv4 users work fine.

MX record — controls where email is delivered

The MX (Mail Exchange) record specifies which server receives email for your domain. It contains a priority (lower = higher priority) and a hostname. A missing or misconfigured MX record means email to your domain bounces or is silently dropped.

When to look it up:

  • Inbound email isn't arriving
  • You just migrated email providers and need to verify the new MX records are in place
  • You're troubleshooting a customer complaint about bounced email
  • You want to confirm your spam filtering service (e.g., Proofpoint, Mimecast) is correctly inserted in the mail flow

TXT record — verification, SPF, DKIM, DMARC

TXT records are used for a wide range of purposes, but the most important in practice are email authentication policies and domain verification tokens:

  • SPF — defines which servers are allowed to send email on behalf of your domain. A missing or overly permissive SPF record increases the chance your legitimate email is marked as spam.
  • DKIM — stores the public key used to verify cryptographic signatures on outgoing email. If DKIM signatures stop verifying, email clients may mark your messages as unverified.
  • DMARC — defines what receiving servers should do with emails that fail SPF or DKIM checks.
  • Domain verification tokens — used by Google Search Console, AWS, Stripe, and other services to confirm you own the domain.

TXT records are frequently the culprit when email deliverability drops suddenly — an SPF record got deleted, a DKIM selector wasn't configured after switching email providers, or a DMARC policy was set to p=reject prematurely.

CNAME record — alias for another hostname

A CNAME (Canonical Name) record maps one hostname to another. Instead of pointing www.example.com directly to an IP, you point it to example.com (or to a CDN hostname like xyz.cloudfront.net). The resolver then looks up that target hostname to get the actual IP.

When to look it up:

  • A subdomain isn't resolving correctly
  • You're verifying a CDN or Cloudflare configuration
  • A service stopped working after a deployment changed a CNAME target
  • You need to find the upstream hostname behind a subdomain for debugging
CNAME at the root zone You can't use a CNAME on a bare domain (e.g., example.com itself) according to DNS standards. Some DNS providers offer "CNAME flattening" or "ALIAS records" that work around this. If you need to point your root domain to a CDN or load balancer, look for these options in your DNS provider's settings.

NS record — identifies your nameservers

NS (Name Server) records identify the authoritative nameservers for your domain. These are the servers that hold the actual DNS records. If your NS records are wrong, your entire domain stops resolving for everyone — nothing works.

NS records are typically set at your domain registrar (not your DNS provider), and they change when you switch DNS providers. When migrating DNS, always verify that your registrar is pointing to the new nameservers before making any other changes.

Command line with dig

dig is available by default on Linux and macOS, and installable on Windows via WSL or BIND tools:

# Look up all common record types
dig example.com A
dig example.com AAAA
dig example.com MX
dig example.com TXT
dig example.com CNAME
dig example.com NS

# Short output (answer only)
dig example.com MX +short

# Query a specific resolver
dig @1.1.1.1 example.com A

nslookup (Windows/macOS)

nslookup -type=MX example.com
nslookup -type=TXT example.com
nslookup -type=NS example.com

What to do after a lookup

If you find a record that's wrong, the fix is straightforward: update it in your DNS provider's dashboard. The time it takes to propagate depends on the TTL set on the record — see how DNS propagation works for details on timing.

If you can't find a record that should be there, check whether it was accidentally deleted (most DNS providers keep a change log), and whether you're looking at the right zone (some providers have separate zones for subdomains).

If you find a record that looks wrong but you didn't change it — treat it as an incident. Unauthorized DNS changes are a real attack vector. Lock down DNS provider access and audit who made the change.

Continuous DNS monitoring

A one-time lookup tells you what your DNS looks like right now. But DNS records change — sometimes intentionally (deployments, migrations), sometimes accidentally (provider errors, unauthorized changes). The only way to catch unintended changes before users do is continuous monitoring.
