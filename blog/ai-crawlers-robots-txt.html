I checked 20 popular developer platforms' robots.txt — most aren't blocking AI crawlers correctly

The data behind the assumption that your robots.txt is protecting you.

This week there's been a lot of discussion about AI scraping — Microsoft execs calling it theft, site owners dealing with unexpected infrastructure costs, developers trying to figure out if their robots.txt is actually doing anything useful.

I got curious and ran a systematic check on 20 popular developer platforms. The results were more interesting than I expected.

Summary
  • 6/20 have zero AI bot rules — heroku, stripe, linear, notion, turso, upstash
  • 7/20 use only Content-Signal instead of per-bot user-agent rules — vercel, railway, planetscale, clerk, convex, neon, fly
  • 5/20 have partial coverage — missing meta-externalagent, the crawler most cited for bill spikes
  • Only 2/20 have comprehensive coverage across all major AI crawlers — render.com and postman.com

The finding that surprised me most: vercel.com itself

Vercel's own marketing site uses Content-Signal but has no explicit per-bot user-agent rules. The platform whose users have been most vocal about AI crawler bill spikes doesn't block the individual bots via robots.txt on its own marketing site.

That's not a criticism of Vercel specifically — it's evidence that even sophisticated teams miss these. The rules keep getting added as new crawlers emerge, and there's no single canonical reference list.

Why Content-Signal doesn't actually block crawlers

Several platforms use the newer Content-Signal directive:

Content-Signal: search=yes, ai-input=yes, ai-train=no

This is a proposed standard that signals your preference — it's advisory, not blocking. Crawlers that honor User-agent rules won't automatically honor Content-Signal. And crawlers that don't honor robots.txt at all won't honor either.

Content-Signal is useful as a signal of intent, but it doesn't replace explicit user-agent rules.

The meta-externalagent gap is the biggest miss

meta-externalagent is Meta's web crawler (Facebook, Instagram, and AI training). It's the bot most frequently cited in discussions about unexpected Vercel and Netlify bill spikes.

Of the 20 platforms I checked, only render.com and postman.com explicitly block it. Even platforms that do block GPTBot and ClaudeBot consistently miss meta-externalagent.

The robots.txt false sense of security

Even when you have rules, there are common gaps:

# Most people know to add this:
User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

# But what about these?
User-agent: ClaudeBot
User-agent: meta-externalagent    ← the one causing bill spikes
User-agent: Bytespider
User-agent: PerplexityBot
User-agent: CCBot
User-agent: Amazonbot
User-agent: DuckAssistBot

There are also case sensitivity issues — User-agent: gptbot doesn't match GPTBot in some parsers. And robots.txt only works for crawlers that choose to honor it.

The infrastructure cost angle

One thing that surprises people: even when bots are blocked by robots.txt, the request still hits your server before your app can respond. On Vercel or Netlify with per-request billing, high-volume crawlers create infrastructure costs regardless of whether they're "blocked" by advisory rules.

That's why monitoring matters alongside blocking — knowing which bot is causing what percentage of your bill lets you respond before the invoice arrives.
