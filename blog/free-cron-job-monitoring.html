Free Cron Job Monitoring: Tools That Alert You When Jobs Fail

A cron job that fails silently costs you more than a paid monitor. Here's what's actually free — and what isn't.

When a cron job fails, nothing happens. No email, no Slack message, no red badge in a dashboard. The job just doesn't do what it was supposed to do, and you find out weeks later when someone notices a report hasn't updated or a backup turns out to be empty.

Free cron job monitoring tools exist specifically to close this gap — adding a dead man's switch that alerts you when a job misses its window. But "free" means different things across different tools, and the limitations matter. This guide breaks down what's actually available at no cost, what the tradeoffs are, and when it makes sense to pay.

1 Why you need cron job monitoring

Cron is intentionally minimal. It runs commands on a schedule and captures output into a local mail queue that, on most modern servers, nobody ever reads. When a job fails — or never runs at all — there is no default notification mechanism.

The real-world cost of this silence is higher than it looks:

  • Missed backups. A backup job that stopped running three months ago means your last restore point is three months old. You find out during an outage.
  • Billing and sync failures. Scheduled tasks that process invoices, sync data between systems, or update subscription status create compounding errors when they silently stop. The longer the outage, the harder the cleanup.
  • Report staleness. Dashboards and reports fed by cron jobs show data that stops updating. Users quietly lose trust in the numbers before anyone reports a bug.
  • Security gaps. Certificate renewal jobs (certbot, acme.sh), security scanning jobs, and log rotation jobs fail silently until something breaks in a visible way.

The fix is straightforward: a monitoring service that expects a signal from your job on a schedule and alerts you when the signal doesn't arrive. This is called a dead man's switch or heartbeat monitor. Many tools offer this for free, with limitations.

healthchecks.io — most popular free option

healthchecks.io is the de-facto standard for free cron job monitoring. The free tier gives you up to 20 monitors and 1 month of log retention. Setup is the classic dead man's switch pattern: you get a unique URL, you append a curl call to your cron job, and healthchecks.io alerts you if the ping doesn't arrive in the expected window.

# Add this to the end of your cron job
0 2 * * * /usr/local/bin/backup.sh && curl -fsS --retry 3 https://hc-ping.com/your-uuid

The main limitation of the free tier is alerting channels: email alerts are included, but Slack, PagerDuty, SMS, and webhook integrations require a paid plan. For a solo developer who checks email, the free tier is genuinely useful.

Cronitor — limited free tier

Cronitor offers monitoring with a polished UI and decent documentation. The free tier caps you at 5 monitors, which is enough to get started but is quickly exhausted on any real project. Most of the useful alert integrations (Slack, PagerDuty, OpsGenie) are paid features. Fine for evaluating the product; not a durable free solution. DIY shell script + email — free but brittle

Writing a wrapper script that captures exit codes and sends an email on failure is a common first attempt at cron monitoring. It's genuinely free and catches job failures — but it has two fundamental problems:

  • It doesn't catch missed runs. If your job never starts — because the server rebooted, the crontab was clobbered, or the daemon stopped — your wrapper script never fires. The most dangerous failure mode goes undetected.
  • It requires a working mail server. Most servers don't have a functional outbound mail configuration. Setting one up is a non-trivial project, and keeping it working (deliverability, spam filters, TLS) is ongoing maintenance.

A DIY alerting script is better than nothing, but it is not a substitute for a proper dead man's switch monitor.

GitHub Actions notifications — a workaround, not monitoring

GitHub sends email notifications when scheduled workflow runs fail. This is useful if all your jobs run as GitHub Actions scheduled workflows — but it's not cron job monitoring in the meaningful sense. It doesn't cover crontab jobs, Heroku Scheduler jobs, Kubernetes CronJobs, or any other scheduler. And it still doesn't give you missed-run detection for GitHub's own scheduled workflows, which are notoriously unreliable during periods of low activity.

3 When free tools hit their limits

The free tiers described above work well for small setups — a handful of jobs, one developer, email alerts are fine. They start to strain at predictable pressure points:

  • Monitor count. healthchecks.io's 20-monitor limit covers a small project. A typical production application has 20–50 scheduled jobs once you include backups, sync tasks, cleanup jobs, billing jobs, report generation, certificate renewal, and health checks.
  • Alert channels. Email-only alerting means you get notified during business hours when you happen to read email. Slack, PagerDuty, and SMS integrations are on paid plans at every major provider. For on-call production jobs, email is often not enough.
  • Retention. One month of log retention makes it hard to spot long-running degradation patterns — jobs that are getting slower over weeks, jobs that fail inconsistently once every few weeks, or duration anomalies that indicate data growth problems.
  • Team access. Free tiers are typically single-user. As soon as two engineers need visibility into cron job status, you need a paid plan.
  • SSL and uptime. Free-hosted services sometimes have worse availability SLAs or maintenance windows that conflict with the jobs they're supposed to be monitoring.
The right question isn't "is free good enough?" — it's "what does a missed job actually cost?" For a backup job that protects weeks of user data, the cost of a missed alert vastly outweighs the cost of even the most expensive monitoring plan. Free tiers make sense for low-stakes jobs; for anything business-critical, price the monitoring against the cost of the failure.

5 How to get started with cron job monitoring

Regardless of which tool you use, the setup pattern is the same. Here's how to go from zero monitoring to active dead man's switch detection in under five minutes:

  1. Pick a tool. For free, start with healthchecks.io. If you need more monitors or Slack alerts, join the CronWatch waitlist.
  2. Create a check. In your monitoring tool, create a new check. Set the schedule (or period), the grace period (how late a job can be before it's considered missed), and your alert channels.
  3. Validate your cron expression first. Before wiring up monitoring, make sure your schedule expression actually fires when you expect. Use the cron expression parser to see the plain-English description and next run times.
  4. Add the ping to your job. Append a curl call to the end of your cron command using && so it only fires on success: 
    0 2 * * * /usr/local/bin/backup.sh && curl -fsS --retry 3 https://monitor-url/your-token
  5. Verify the first ping arrives. Run the job manually or wait for the next scheduled run. Confirm the check flips to "up" in your monitoring dashboard before relying on it.
  6. Test the alert. Temporarily set the period shorter than your job's normal run interval, or disable the job, and confirm you receive an alert when the ping doesn't arrive. An untested alert is not a reliable alert.

Once the first job is instrumented, the pattern takes less than a minute per job to replicate. The investment compounds quickly as you add monitoring to backup jobs, sync jobs, and billing tasks that have been running silently for months.
